Most contractors treat the 401(k) as a December job. The year is winding down, the jobs are finishing up, and someone says it's time to think about retirement contributions. By then, the best options have usually closed. The safe harbor decision is made in the fall, sometimes earlier, and the cost of waiting shows up in three places: the plan design you can still choose, the deduction you can still take, and how much of your own money you can actually put away.

This post walks through how the timing works for a calendar-year plan, what it looks like on a typical construction payroll, and which SECURE 2.0 changes are worth checking before you sign anything. The dollar limits below are the 2025 figures. They adjust every year, so confirm the current numbers on the [IRS retirement plans page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans) or with us before you commit.

## Why a failed test lands on the owner first

A standard 401(k) has to pass nondiscrimination testing every year. The deferral test (ADP) compares what your highly compensated employees (HCEs) defer, as a percentage of pay, against what everyone else defers. You are an HCE if you own more than 5% of the business, or if you were paid above $160,000 the prior year (the 2025 threshold).

Here is the problem on a construction payroll. Field crews, laborers, and apprentices often defer little or nothing, and plenty of them are living job to job. If your non-HCE group averages 2%, the HCE group generally can't average more than 4%.

Say you pay yourself $250,000. At a 4% cap, you can defer $10,000. The 2025 employee deferral limit is $23,500. That leaves $13,500 of your own contribution room stuck behind a test your crew's paychecks are controlling.

If the plan fails outright, the plan has to refund the excess deferrals to HCEs, generally by March 15 for a calendar-year plan, to avoid a 10% excise tax on the employer. Those refunds are taxable to you. You lose the deferral, and you pay tax on the money coming back.

A safe harbor plan skips the ADP test, and the related matching test (ACP) for the safe harbor contributions. In exchange, you commit to a required employer contribution. That is the trade, and it is why the design choice has to be made before the plan year runs out.

## Safe harbor match vs nonelective contribution

There are two main ways to fund a safe harbor plan. Each has a different cost profile and, more importantly, a different deadline.

| | Basic safe harbor match | Safe harbor nonelective |
|---|---|---|
| Employer contribution | 100% of the first 3% an employee defers, plus 50% of the next 2% (4% of pay at most) | 3% of pay to every eligible employee, whether they defer or not |
| Who gets it | Only employees who defer | Everyone eligible |
| Annual notice to employees | Required | Not required |
| Vesting | Immediate (a QACA design can use a 2-year cliff) | Immediate (a QACA design can use a 2-year cliff) |
| Late adoption | Generally not available | Available, with limits |

Here is what that costs on a sample contractor with $1,000,000 of eligible payroll: you at $250,000 and 12 employees at $750,000 combined.

| Scenario | Estimated employer cost |
|---|---|
| Nonelective 3%, anyone defers or not | $30,000 |
| Basic match, you plus 3 employees defer 5% or more | about $17,500 |
| Basic match, you plus 6 employees defer 5% or more | about $25,000 |
| Basic match, you plus all 12 employees defer 5% or more | about $40,000 |

The match is cheaper when few people defer, and it gets more expensive as your crew participates more. The nonelective is a fixed number. It also puts 3% in the paycheck-to-paycheck employees' accounts whether or not they ever sign up, which tends to help with retention in a trade where good people leave for another contractor over a dollar an hour.

One more detail on the nonelective. Your own 3%, $7,500 on $250,000, goes in your account too. Add your $23,500 deferral and you are at $31,000, well under the $70,000 overall limit for 2025, so there is room for additional profit sharing if the business had a strong year. If you are 50 or older, catch-up contributions go on top.

## The dates that matter for a calendar-year plan

The notice and adoption rules are where owners get caught. The short version:

- **Before the plan year starts (match plans):** Eligible employees must receive a safe harbor notice 30 to 90 days before the year begins. For a January 1 plan, that window is roughly October 2 through December 2 of the prior year.
- **Nonelective plans:** The SECURE Act eliminated the notice requirement for plans that use only the 3% nonelective safe harbor. Employees still have to be allowed to make or change elections each year.
- **Late nonelective adoption:** Under the SECURE Act, you can adopt the 3% nonelective safe harbor for an existing plan any time up to 30 days before the plan year ends. For a calendar-year plan, that is around December 1.
- **Even later:** After that point, you can still adopt a safe harbor for the year by making a 4% nonelective contribution, as late as the end of the following plan year.
- **Brand-new plan:** A new plan generally needs at least three months in its first plan year to qualify as safe harbor. In practice, an October 1 start for a calendar-year plan is the usual cutoff, and a provider's own setup timeline can push it earlier.
- **Funding and deduction:** Employer contributions are generally deductible for the year if deposited by your tax return due date, including extensions. Your employees' own deferrals still have to come out of actual paychecks during the year.

That last point is the reason waiting hurts. The deferral window closes with the final payroll of the year. A match plan can't be bolted on in December. The cheaper late-adoption route, the 3% nonelective, ends around December 1. After that, you are paying 4% for the same result.

Providers and payroll systems have their own cutoffs, and plan documents sometimes need to be amended before you act. Check your plan document and your provider's deadlines before relying on any date in this post.

## Seasonal crews, union hours, and prevailing wage jobs

Construction payroll has quirks that generic plan-provider material doesn't cover. They affect who counts, what you owe, and how the contribution is treated on public jobs.

![Construction crew working on a job site](/content-assets/construction-crew-on-job-site.jpg)

**Seasonal and part-time workers.** Eligibility rules decide who is in the test and who gets the safe harbor contribution. Under the SECURE Act and SECURE 2.0, long-term part-time employees who work at least 500 hours a year for two consecutive years (phased in from 2024 and 2025) generally have to be allowed to defer. A crew you bring on every April and let go in November can trip this over time, so track hours.

**Union employees.** Workers covered by a collective bargaining agreement that provides for retirement benefits can generally be excluded from your 401(k) and from the testing. Your union pension or annuity contributions are a separate obligation, and they need to be coordinated with the plan, not stacked blindly on top of it.

**Public works.** On prevailing wage jobs, certain bona fide fringe benefit contributions can count toward the fringe portion of the required wage rate. A 401(k) contribution may help here, but only if it is handled properly on certified payroll. Our [payroll compliance team](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/payroll) works with contractors on public works contracts and can show you how the numbers fit before you set the contribution formula.

**Multiple entities.** Many contractor owners run a construction company, a separate equipment or real estate entity, and sometimes a management company. Related businesses can be treated as one employer for plan purposes, which changes who has to be covered. Shawn Wilson, CPA, handles multi-entity tax planning and cash-flow consulting for owners of construction and hospitality businesses, and this is one of the first things he checks.

## SECURE 2.0 changes to check before you adopt

SECURE 2.0 added several rules that touch plan design. Confirm each against current IRS guidance, because effective dates and limits shift:

- **Automatic enrollment for new plans.** New 401(k) plans established after December 29, 2022 generally must automatically enroll eligible employees starting with plan years that begin after 2024. Businesses with 10 or fewer employees and businesses less than three years old are exempt. A plan that is already in place is generally grandfathered.
- **Startup credits.** Employers with 50 or fewer employees may be able to claim a credit for up to 100% of startup costs, capped at $5,000 a year for three years, plus a credit for employer contributions. Confirm eligibility with your CPA before relying on either.
- **Roth catch-up for higher earners.** Beginning in 2026, employees age 50 and over who were paid more than roughly $150,000 in Social Security wages the prior year (indexed) must make catch-up contributions as Roth. If you pay yourself a W-2 salary in that range, this applies to you.
- **Higher catch-up at ages 60 to 63.** The limit for this age band is the greater of $10,000 or 150% of the regular catch-up amount, which is $11,250 for 2025. If you are within a few years of that age band, it can change how much you plan to put in.
- **Owner-only businesses.** A newer rule lets a sole proprietor with no employees (other than a spouse) make first-year employee deferrals after the plan year ends, up to the tax filing deadline. It does not apply once you have a crew on the payroll.

The safe harbor contribution can sit alongside most of these provisions, but they affect your plan document, your payroll setup, and your timeline. That is why the work belongs in September or October, not the week of Christmas.

## A fall checklist for contractors

If you want a simple order of operations, this is the one we use with construction clients at our [retirement plan analysis](/what-we-do/advisory/retirement-plan-analysis) meetings:

1. **September:** Run a projected ADP test using year-to-date payroll. If it fails, you want to know in September.
2. **Early October:** Price the match against the nonelective, using your real crew participation and headcount, not a rule of thumb.
3. **Before December 1:** Adopt the 3% nonelective by plan amendment if you are going that route, and update your payroll so the owner deferral is in place.
4. **Before year-end:** Check the owner's deferral total against the limit, including catch-up.
5. **Before your return due date:** Deposit the employer contribution and confirm the deduction on the business return.

This fits with the rest of your tax picture, too. The employer contribution is a business deduction that changes your estimated payments, and it interacts with your entity choice, since owner pay looks different in an S-corp than in a sole proprietorship. ## Common Questions

**What is the safe harbor 401(k) deadline?**

For a calendar-year plan, match plans need a notice 30 to 90 days before the year begins. A 3% nonelective safe harbor can generally be adopted until about 30 days before year-end (around December 1). After that, a 4% nonelective can still qualify, up to the end of the following plan year.

**Do I need to send a notice for a nonelective safe harbor plan?**

No. The SECURE Act removed the annual notice requirement for plans that use only the nonelective safe harbor contribution. Employees must still have a chance to make or change their elections, and plans with a safe harbor match still need the notice. Check your plan document in case it adds its own requirements.

**Can I still set up a safe harbor plan in December?**

For a brand-new plan, usually not. A new plan generally needs three months in its first plan year, so October 1 is a typical cutoff. An existing plan can still add the 3% nonelective until about December 1, or the 4% nonelective after that, which is why the fall is the planning window.
