It's mid-December, the year is winding down, and your desk has a pile on it. A workers' comp renewal notice. A lease that rolls over next month. A license card with a date you can't quite read. Most business owners we talk to in Port Chester are good at running their business and bad at remembering which paperwork dates are coming. That isn't a character flaw. Those dates are scattered across brokers, state websites, and old email threads, and nobody hands them to you in one place.

A business compliance calendar fixes that. It is one page, built once a year, that shows every renewal, filing, and notice deadline for the next twelve months. December is the best time to build it, because the slow stretch gives you room to think and the dates that matter most hit in January and February.

## Why December works for business owners

Most businesses have a rhythm, and for many in Westchester, Fairfield County, and the five boroughs, things ease off between Christmas and the first week of January. That gap is when you can sit down for two or three hours without constant interruptions.

There's also a practical reason. Many of the deadlines that cost business owners money are not in April. They're in the first 60 days of the year: W-2 and 1099 filings, fourth-quarter payroll returns, insurance renewals, and the first state reports of the year. If you map them in December, you walk into January knowing what's coming. If you don't, you find out when a penalty notice arrives.

The goal isn't to create a binder nobody opens. It's to turn a handful of recurring surprises into dates on a calendar, with a reminder set far enough ahead to act on them.

## Review insurance 60 to 90 days before it renews

Insurance is the first thing to put on the calendar, and it's the one most business owners review too late. By the time the renewal quote shows up, you have a week to decide, and the easy move is to say yes.

Start by listing every policy with its renewal date:

- General liability
- Workers' compensation
- Commercial auto
- Umbrella or excess liability
- Property insurance for your space and equipment
- Professional liability, bonds, or other coverage your contracts require

Then ask your broker for renewal quotes 60 to 90 days out, not 10. That gives you time to compare options and ask why a premium moved.

Workers' comp deserves extra attention because the premium is built on payroll and job classification codes. If your payroll grew, or you moved an employee into a supervisory role, the numbers your carrier has may no longer match reality. Insurers audit these figures after the policy year, and a bad estimate can turn into a surprise bill. Your bookkeeper or CPA can pull payroll by classification in minutes, so you hand your broker real figures instead of guesses.

Also check your certificates of insurance. Customers, landlords, and lenders often require specific limits and additional-insured language, and a lapse can hold up a payment or a new contract. If coverage looks heavier than your actual risk, or thinner, our post on [sizing insurance coverage to your actual risk](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl) walks through how to think about it.

## Read the contracts that renew themselves

Auto-renewals are quiet. An equipment rental, a software subscription, a supplier agreement, or the lease on your office or storage space can roll into another full year if you miss a cancellation window.

Here's a simple example. Say you lease a delivery van for $1,100 a month under a 12-month agreement that renews automatically unless you give 60 days' written notice. If business slowed in October and you forgot the notice date, that's $13,200 for a van sitting in the lot. The date that matters isn't the end of the term. It's the notice deadline, and that's the one to put on your calendar.

Go through each agreement and record three things: the end date, the notice date, and what it costs to leave. Then ask whether the terms still fit how you work. Vendors often move on pricing or payment terms when you ask early, especially if you've paid on time all year.

Vendor and customer agreements deserve the same look. Check payment terms, late fees, and language about changes in scope. If a customer pays in 75 days while your suppliers want 30, you're financing their business. Our post on [firing unprofitable customers and renegotiating vendor contracts](/resources/its-not-you-its-your-margins-how-to-break-up-with-unprofitab) covers how to bring that up without burning the relationship.

## Gather licenses, permits, and certifications

Licenses are easy to forget because they renew on cycles that don't match anything else in your year. Some renew annually, some every two years, and the issuer depends on where you work: a village, a county, a city, or the state.

![Business owner reviewing license and permit documents at a desk](/content-assets/contractor-reviewing-permit-job-site.jpg)

Make a short list of everything that lets you work legally and win business:

- Business and professional licenses, with the issuing office and expiration date
- Local permits for your location or the services you offer
- Safety or industry training certifications your work requires
- Any certifications your customers or clients require

Put each expiration date on the calendar with a reminder 90 days ahead. Renewals sometimes need proof of insurance, so they connect back to the first step. The SBA keeps a useful overview of [applying for licenses and permits](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits) if you're unsure which levels of government apply to a new service line. Rules differ by municipality, so confirm the details with the office that issues each one.

## Put state reports and payroll filings on the same page

This is where the details matter most. If you run a business entity in New York, New Jersey, or Connecticut, each state has its own report schedule. New York LLCs file a biennial statement, New Jersey entities file an annual report, and Connecticut has its own annual report. If you work across state lines, you may have more than one.

Payroll adds another layer. Federal and state payroll returns, year-end wage statements, and unemployment filings all have fixed dates, and each state you have employees in adds its own rules. Our [payroll compliance support](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/payroll) covers payroll tax filings across New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut, and Nicholas Vergara on our team handles payroll tax filing for employers with multiple locations or seasonal swings.

Here is what the first quarter often looks like for a Port Chester business owner with employees and a pass-through entity:

| Timing | What's due | Who it touches |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-January | Fourth-quarter estimated tax payment | Owners of S-corps, partnerships, and sole proprietorships |
| January 31 | W-2s, 1099-NECs, fourth-quarter payroll returns | Anyone with employees or paid contractors |
| Mid-March | S-corp and partnership tax returns | Pass-through entities |
| Anniversary month | State annual report or biennial statement | Entities formed or registered in NY, NJ, or CT |

These are typical dates, and they shift when a deadline lands on a weekend. Confirm each one for your entity type, or let us confirm it for you. The [IRS small business and self-employed page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good place to check the federal side.

One more reason to get 1099s right: if your 1099 records aren't clean, you're chasing W-9s in late January with a deadline already running. Collect them now, from every contractor or vendor you paid this year.

## Add the tax dates that touch your cash

A compliance calendar and a tax plan belong together. A few decisions only exist until December 31, so they should be on the calendar before the holidays.

Equipment is the big one. If you're thinking about a vehicle or a major piece of equipment, the date it's placed in service can change what you deduct for the year. That's worth a conversation before you sign, and it ties directly to how you finance it. Our post on [leasing vs. buying a vehicle](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) covers the cash flow side.

Retirement plans are the other. Some plan options have notice deadlines well before year-end, and a safe harbor 401(k) is the clearest example. If you missed it, put next fall's date on this year's calendar now. Our post on [the safe harbor 401(k) deadline business owners miss every fall](/resources/the-safe-harbor-401-k-deadline-business-owners-often-miss-wh) explains the timing.

Finally, look at your fourth-quarter estimate. With income that swings through the year, a profitable fall can leave you underpaid. Shawn Wilson, CPA works with owners who run more than one entity, and he'll tell you the same thing: projecting the year in December costs a lot less than correcting it in April.

## Build it in one afternoon

You don't need software. A shared calendar and a spreadsheet will do. Here's the order we'd follow:

1. Pull every renewal letter, license, lease, and contract into one folder.
2. Write down two dates for each item: the actual deadline and the date you need to start acting.
3. Add the state report dates for every entity you own.
4. Add payroll, 1099, and estimated tax dates from your CPA.
5. Set reminders 30, 60, or 90 days ahead, depending on how much decision time each item needs.
6. Name one person who owns the calendar, even if that's you.

If you're a client, much of the information is already in your secure online platform: payroll history, vendor payments, and prior filings. That makes step one much faster, since we can pull the numbers rather than asking you to dig for them.

## Common Questions

**What is a business compliance calendar?**

It's a single schedule of every recurring deadline your business has: insurance renewals, license expirations, contract notice dates, payroll filings, state reports, and tax payments. For a contractor, it also covers certified payroll and bonding renewals. The value is the reminder dates, set early enough that you can compare options and decide.

**When should I review my business insurance before renewal?**

Start 60 to 90 days before the policy ends. That gives your broker time to shop quotes and gives you time to update payroll, equipment, and revenue figures. For workers' comp, bring payroll by job classification, since the premium is based on it.

**Do I need a calendar if I only operate in New York?**

Yes. Even a single-state contractor has federal payroll returns, state unemployment filings, a state entity report, local licenses, and several insurance policies. If you also take jobs in Connecticut or New Jersey, you may have extra registration and filing requirements, so the calendar matters more.
