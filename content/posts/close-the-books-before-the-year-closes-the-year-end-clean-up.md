Most contractors don't dread tax season because of the tax. They dread the first week of January, when the preparer opens the books and finds a year of uncategorized transactions, a bank account nobody has reconciled since spring, and a truck payment coded as materials. Every hour spent untangling that mess shows up on your invoice.

The fix is cheaper than the problem, and it fits in Q4. This year-end bookkeeping checklist for taxes walks through the four cleanup jobs that matter most for construction businesses in Port Chester and the surrounding Westchester, Fairfield, and New York City job markets: reconciling accounts, separating personal spending, correcting miscategorized transactions, and tightening up subcontractor and payroll records.

## Why messy books raise your tax prep bill

A tax return is only as good as the books behind it. When the books are clean, your preparer starts with a trial balance and works on tax strategy. When they are not, the first stretch of the engagement is detective work: chasing down what a $4,800 debit was, figuring out whether a transfer was a loan or a draw, and asking you to remember a supplier from February.

That detective time is billable under most hourly arrangements. It also delays the part you actually want, which is a conversation about what you owe and how to lower it while there is still time to act.

Here is a simple example. Picture a remodeling contractor with six accounts: one operating checking account, a job-deposit account, two credit cards, an equipment loan, and a line of credit. If those accounts were never reconciled, a preparer might spend a full day just matching statements to the ledger before touching a single tax line. Do that matching in November and it takes a bookkeeper a couple of hours, because you still remember what the charges were.

The [IRS recordkeeping guidance for small businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping) says the same thing in plainer terms: your records need to support the income and deductions on your return. Clean books are how you show that.

## Reconcile every account before December 31

Reconciling means matching what your books say to what the bank, card company, or lender says, line by line, until the ending balances agree. It sounds basic. It is also the step most often skipped when jobs get busy.

![Bookkeeper matching bank statements to accounting software with a calculator](/content-assets/bookkeeper-reconciling-bank-statements.jpg)

Start with these accounts and work through each one for every month of the year:

- Operating checking and any separate job or deposit accounts
- Business credit cards, including cards held by foremen or project managers
- Equipment, truck, and vehicle loans (check that principal and interest are split correctly)
- Lines of credit
- Payroll clearing or payroll liability accounts
- Merchant or payment platforms like Square, Stripe, or PayPal, if clients pay you that way

Watch for the things that quietly throw a reconciliation off. Deposits sitting in transit at year-end, checks written to suppliers that haven't cleared, duplicate bank feed entries in QuickBooks, and a customer payment applied to the wrong invoice are the usual suspects. If a number won't tie out, flag it now. A difference of $300 in October is a five-minute question. The same difference in March is a mystery.

## Separate personal spending from the business

Commingling is the most common cleanup problem in small construction companies, and it is easy to see why. You stop at the lumber yard on the way home and grab a few things for the house. The company card pays for a family dinner because it was in your pocket. The truck is used for jobs and weekend errands.

None of that is unusual. It just has to be sorted out before the return is prepared, because personal expenses deducted as business expenses are a problem if anyone ever asks questions.

Here is a practical way to handle it in December:

1. Scan each business account and card for personal charges: groceries, family travel, home utilities, streaming services, school costs.
2. Move them out of expense categories. If the business paid, record them as owner draws (sole proprietors and partnerships) or shareholder distributions or loans to the owner (corporations). Your preparer can tell you which treatment fits your entity.
3. For vehicles used both ways, reconstruct a mileage log from calendars, job addresses, and invoices. Business use percentage drives how much of the truck is deductible.
4. If you paid a business bill from a personal account, record it as owner contribution or reimbursement instead of leaving it off the books.
5. Going forward, pick one card for the business and one for personal use. Stick to it, even when it is inconvenient.

Owners with a construction company plus a separate entity, such as an LLC that holds a building or equipment, have an extra layer to watch. Money moving between entities needs a paper trail, and multi-entity planning is something Shawn Wilson, CPA, works through with owners regularly.

## Fix the miscategorized transactions that distort job costs

Bad categories cost you twice. They muddy the tax return, and they hide which jobs actually make money. Open your profit and loss report and look for these red flags:

| What you see | What it usually means | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Large "Uncategorized" or "Ask my accountant" balance | Bank feed items nobody reviewed | The right expense, income, or balance sheet account |
| Truck or equipment purchase in "Repairs" or "Supplies" | Asset coded as an expense | Fixed assets, then depreciation or Section 179 review |
| Loan payment fully in expenses | Principal treated as a cost | Principal reduces the loan; only interest is an expense |
| Owner transfers in "Wages" or "Job expenses" | Draws mixed into operating costs | Owner draw or distribution |
| Materials and subcontractor costs lumped together | No job-level detail | Separate cost of goods sold accounts, tagged by job |
| Customer deposits recorded as income immediately | Billing ahead of work | Deferred revenue or contract liability, depending on your method |

The equipment row deserves extra attention in Q4. If you bought a truck, excavator, or other equipment this year, it generally needs to be placed in service by December 31 to be considered for first-year expensing. Whether Section 179 or bonus depreciation makes sense depends on your income and cash position, which is exactly the kind of decision that is easier to make in November than in April.

Then look at job costing. If labor, materials, and subs are not tagged by job, you cannot see that the $85,000 kitchen job cleared a 22 percent margin while the $40,000 deck job barely broke even. Fixing categories now gives you real numbers to price next year's work. ## Check your subcontractor and payroll paperwork

Construction has more paperwork exposure than most industries, and it all comes due in January.

![Construction crew working on a building site](/content-assets/construction-crew-on-job-site.jpg)

**Subcontractors.** Make sure you have a signed W-9 for every sub you paid this year. If one is missing, request it now, while the relationship is warm and the final payment hasn't gone out. Form 1099-NEC is due to recipients and the IRS by January 31, and chasing a sub for a tax ID in late January is nobody's idea of fun. Also confirm each sub's certificate of insurance is on file, since a lapse can create liability and workers' compensation headaches.

**Payroll.** Reconcile your payroll reports to your books. Wages, withholdings, and employer taxes in the ledger should match your quarterly Form 941 filings and your New York quarterly filings. W-2s are also due January 31, so any employee address changes or classification questions should be settled before the last payroll of the year.

**Public works jobs.** If you work on public contracts, certified payroll and prevailing wage reports need to agree with what you actually paid. Mismatches between certified payroll records and the general ledger are easy to create and annoying to explain later. Our [payroll team](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/payroll) handles certified payroll and prevailing wage compliance support for contractors, so those records line up with the books.

Owner retirement contributions are the last piece. Some plan deadlines, such as the safe harbor 401(k) notice, arrive well before December. ## A Q4 timeline that fits a contractor's schedule

Year-end cleanup goes better in small pieces than in one weekend marathon. Here is a pace that works around a busy fall season.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| October | Reconcile January through September for every account. Collect missing W-9s and insurance certificates. |
| Early November | Review the profit and loss report for uncategorized items, personal charges, and assets coded as expenses. |
| Late November | Reconcile October. Run job cost reports for open jobs and check for unbilled work and retainage. |
| Early December | Meet with your CPA to project taxable income, decide on equipment purchases, and set estimated payments or retirement contributions. |
| Mid to late December | Make any planned purchases and contributions. Send final payments to subs only after paperwork is complete. |
| Early January | Reconcile December, close the books, and send your 1099 and W-2 information. |

The early December meeting is where cleanup pays off most. With accurate numbers, a CPA can run tax projections and show you what changes if you buy the truck in December versus January. With inaccurate numbers, you are guessing.

## What to hand your accountant in January

Once the books are closed, the handoff is short. A tidy package includes the year-end reconciliations, a final profit and loss report and balance sheet, a fixed asset list with purchase dates and costs, loan statements showing year-end balances, your mileage log, payroll summaries, and your 1099 data. If your firm uses a secure online platform, upload everything there instead of emailing attachments.

That is the whole list. When it is ready, preparation becomes a review, not an excavation, and that is where the fee savings come from.

## Common Questions

**When should a contractor start the year-end bookkeeping cleanup?**

Start in October. That gives you time to reconcile nine months of statements, collect missing subcontractor W-9s, and still have a planning meeting with your CPA in early December. Starting in January leaves you reacting to the numbers instead of using them to make decisions before December 31.

**Does cleaner bookkeeping really lower tax preparation fees?**

Usually, yes. Preparers bill for the time it takes to sort out transactions, match statements, and ask you questions. If your accounts are reconciled and your categories are correct, that time shrinks. The savings are larger when personal expenses are already separated and subcontractor paperwork is complete.

**How do I separate personal and business expenses if I've mixed them all year?**

Review each account and card for personal charges, then record them as owner draws, distributions, or loans to the owner, depending on your entity type. Rebuild a mileage log for any vehicle used both ways. From here on, use one card for the business and one for personal spending.
