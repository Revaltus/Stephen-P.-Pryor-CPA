Every contractor has one customer they wince at when the phone lights up. The one who pays at day 85, disputes the last invoice, and texts at 9 p.m. about a change they approved verbally in October. You keep the job because revenue is revenue and February is slow. But revenue and profit are different things, and some of your customers are quietly costing you money.

Valentine's Day is a handy excuse to take a hard look at who you work with and who you buy from. Keep the relationships that pay off. Renegotiate the ones that drain you. End the ones that will never love your bottom line back. Here is how to do that with real numbers, for a construction business in Port Chester or anywhere in the tri-state area.

## Why winter is the right time to review your customer list

Winter is when many Westchester and Fairfield County contractors have the most breathing room. Fewer crews are stretched across jobs, the year-end books are closing, and the spring bid calendar has not filled up yet. If you are going to turn down a customer, you want to do it before you have committed crews and materials for the season.

It is also when last year's numbers are fresh. Your 2024 or 2025 job-cost reports, aging receivables, and vendor statements hold the answers. You just have to line them up in one place.

## Find the real margin on each customer

Most contractors know their gross margin by job. Fewer know their margin by customer after the hidden costs. A customer profitability analysis adds three things that rarely show up on a job-cost report:

- **Office and project management time.** Pay applications, lien waivers, change-order paperwork, and certified payroll for public work all take hours. Price them at your loaded hourly cost.
- **Rework and back-charges.** Callbacks, disputed deductions, and punch-list items that drag on for months.
- **The cost of waiting to get paid.** If you carry a line of credit at 9 percent, every day a customer sits on your invoice has a price.

Here is what that looks like for three sample customers over one year.

| | Repeat developer | General contractor | Remodel homeowner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $240,000 | $310,000 | $95,000 |
| Direct job costs | $192,000 | $263,500 | $71,250 |
| Gross profit | $48,000 (20%) | $46,500 (15%) | $23,750 (25%) |
| Office and PM time | $2,400 | $7,200 | $8,400 |
| Rework and back-charges | $0 | $14,000 | $7,500 |
| Carrying cost of slow pay | $1,775 | $6,500 | $470 |
| **True profit** | **$43,825** | **$18,800** | **$7,380** |
| **True margin** | **18.3%** | **6.1%** | **7.8%** |

Look at the homeowner. On the job report, that was the best margin on the list at 25 percent. After 140 hours of texts, revisions, and rework, it is the second worst. And the general contractor, your biggest customer by revenue, earns you barely six cents on the dollar while tying up $310,000 of your work for nearly three months at a time.

Those numbers are only as good as your bookkeeping. If costs are not coded to jobs and customers each month, the analysis turns into guesswork. That is why [monthly bookkeeping with job-cost tracking](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/bookkeeping) sits at the center of how we work with contractors.

## Set a floor before you set a feeling

The hardest part of ending a customer relationship is that it feels personal. A written threshold takes some of the emotion out of it.

Pick a minimum true margin. For many contractors it lands somewhere between 10 and 12 percent after overhead is considered, but your number depends on your fixed costs and what you want to take home. Then add a second rule for payment behavior, such as no customer averaging more than 60 days to pay.

With a 10 percent floor, the table above is easy to read. The developer stays, and you probably look for more work like it. The general contractor and the homeowner are both below the line. That does not mean they are gone. It means they get a conversation.

## Try to fix the relationship first

Firing is the last step, not the first. Most underwater customers can be repaired with one or two changes, and you often learn something about your own contracts along the way.

- **Raise your price.** A 5 percent increase on the homeowner job lifts true profit from $7,380 to $12,130, a 64 percent jump. If they leave, you lose very little.
- **Tighten payment terms.** Require a deposit of 25 to 30 percent, move to progress billing every two weeks, and add a late fee that is written in the contract.
- **Enforce a change-order rule.** No extra work starts until a signed change order arrives. Verbal approvals are how $7,500 of rework happens.
- **Set a minimum job size.** Small, scattered jobs for the same customer often cost more per dollar of revenue than one larger job.
- **Negotiate retainage.** Ask for release at substantial completion instead of final closeout.

Give the customer a clear, calm explanation and a deadline. Some will agree and become better customers. The ones who push back on every reasonable term have told you what you need to know.

## How to end a customer relationship without burning the bridge

Port Chester is a small business community. The GC you walk away from today may sit next to your next best customer at a Chamber event, so how you leave matters.

1. **Finish what is under contract.** Do not walk off a job. Complete the current scope, collect what you are owed, and close it properly.
2. **Stop bidding.** The simplest exit is to decline future work or bid at a price that reflects your true cost.
3. **Say it in writing, and keep it short.** Thank them, state that you will not be taking new projects, and give the date your current work wraps up.
4. **Offer a handoff.** A referral to another contractor shows goodwill and cuts the risk of an angry reply.
5. **Check your contract and lien deadlines.** If money is still owed, have your attorney review notice and lien deadlines before you send anything. Missing one can cost you the right to collect.

You do not owe them a long explanation. "Our schedule and pricing no longer fit your projects" is honest, and it ends the conversation.

## What happens to your books and taxes when a customer walks or never pays

This is where the tax side of a breakup shows up.

If you use the accrual method and a customer's invoice becomes truly uncollectible, you may be able to deduct it as a business bad debt. You need to show the amount was already included in income and that you made reasonable efforts to collect. If you are a cash-basis contractor, you generally cannot deduct an unpaid invoice, because you never counted it as income in the first place. You simply do not receive the money.

Retainage deserves special attention. Money held back for a year or more and never released needs to be tracked separately, not buried in accounts receivable. Keep your collection emails, demand letters, and lien filings in one folder. If a deduction is ever questioned, that file is your support. The [IRS small business resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) are a good starting point, and your CPA can confirm how the rules apply to your method of accounting.

## Renegotiate vendor contracts before you cancel them

Customers are only half of your margin. Materials, equipment rentals, subcontractors, insurance, software, and dumpster services make up most of your direct costs. If you have not asked for better terms in two years, you are probably leaving money on the table.

![Stacks of lumber and building materials at a supply yard](/content-assets/lumber-yard-building-supplies.jpg)

Start with a simple list. Pull 12 months of spending by vendor from QuickBooks and sort it from highest to lowest. For most contractors, five or six vendors account for the bulk of the dollars. Those are the ones worth a call.

Then ask for specific things:

- **Volume pricing or a rebate.** If you spent $150,000 at a supply yard last year, say so and ask for a tier.
- **Early-payment discounts.** Terms of 2/10 net 30 mean a 2 percent discount for paying 20 days sooner. That works out to roughly a 37 percent annualized return, far better than what your line of credit costs.
- **Price locks.** Lumber, copper, and fuel move. Ask for a fixed price for a quarter or for a specific project.
- **Delivery guarantees.** Late materials are expensive. Four carpenters waiting for eight hours at a $55 loaded rate is $1,760 gone in a day.
- **Net 45 or net 60 terms.** If your largest customer pays you at 85 days, your vendor terms should not be 15.

Read the contract itself while you are at it. Auto-renewals, minimum purchase commitments, and early termination fees tend to hide in the fine print of equipment rentals, software, and service agreements. If you want to clean up the small stuff too, our post on [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) shows how to audit recurring charges.

## When to terminate a vendor contract

A vendor is worth leaving when the pain is repeatable and they will not fix it. Common triggers:

- Prices climbed more than your own costs justify, and a competing quote is 8 to 10 percent lower.
- Late or incomplete deliveries have delayed at least two jobs in a quarter.
- Quality problems are creating rework you cannot bill for.
- The contract locks you into minimums you no longer meet.

Before you cancel, check the notice window and any exit fee. A $2,500 termination fee can be worth paying if the new vendor saves you $1,000 a month. Termination fees and breakage costs are generally deductible as ordinary business expenses, but the details depend on the contract, so check with your CPA before you sign anything. The [SBA's business management guidance](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) is also useful for vendor planning.

Finally, line up the replacement before you give notice. No contractor wants to find out on a Monday morning that the new supplier cannot deliver until Thursday.

## Common Questions

**How do I fire unprofitable customers without hurting my reputation?**

Finish all work you have under contract, then stop bidding on new projects. Send a short, polite written note that you will not be taking future work and offer a referral if possible. Skip the long explanations. A clean, professional exit rarely damages your standing, and it protects your payment and lien rights.

**What is a good profit margin to keep a construction customer?**

Many contractors set a floor of 10 to 12 percent true margin after office time, rework, and the cost of slow payment. Your number depends on your overhead and income goals. The key is to calculate it by customer, not just by job, so slow payers and high-maintenance accounts show up clearly.

**Can I deduct an unpaid invoice from a customer I stopped working with?**

If you use accrual accounting and the debt is truly uncollectible, you may be able to deduct it as a business bad debt, as long as you can show collection efforts. Cash-basis businesses generally cannot deduct unpaid invoices because the income was never recorded. Keep your demand letters and emails as support.
